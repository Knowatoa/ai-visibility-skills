# Talks

The job is giving a presentation — conference, meetup, webinar, internal
talk — and leaving with something that can live online.

You walk off stage with a recording (if you made one), a deck, and a
set of lines people will repeat. Publish the talk. Chop it into shorts.
Write the recap. Put the same stories on your site. Each of those
surfaces can be crawled, indexed, and used as training data. That is
why a talk belongs in this catalog: not the room, the asset.

Skills in this repo cover the prep through the deck:

- [talk-outline](../skills/talk-outline) — who it is
  for, what they can do on Monday, what you get, then a disposable
  outline
- [talk-storyboard](../skills/talk-storyboard) — the feeling beat by
  beat, then whether that outline is the one to keep
- [talk-slides](../skills/talk-slides) — a sparse PowerPoint cut
  from that storyboard: walk the markdown plan one look at a
  time, then a cue each look, about 15-30 seconds

## How we prepare talks

A lot of that method is inspired by Justin Searls. Watch
[Secrets of Great Conference Talks](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=rOf5sPSBLjg)
(2023). He posted it on [his site](https://justin.searls.co/tubes/2023-12-12-11h08m54s/)
and published a [written version with the transcript](https://testdouble.com/insights/how-to-start-speaking-at-conferences).

The notes below are what we took for this catalog. They are not a
substitute for the video, and they are not his words.

### What the skills already do

**Wait for something worth saying.** If the only reason to speak is
wanting the slot, skip it. `talk-outline` makes you name a
presenter win you could check in 30 days. "Thought leadership" is not
one.

**Give ideas breathing room.** Searls spends a calendar month letting
the talk chew in the background. `talk-outline` defaults to
scratch: throwaway ~10-bullet rounds, then walk away. The forgetting
is the filter. Do not marry the first list.

**Outline, then storyboard, then outline again.** He writes a
lightweight outline, sets it aside, draws a storyboard, and repeats
until the flow feels inevitable. He also reads the outline
adversarially before calling it done. That split is two skills here:
`talk-outline` owns the disposable lists and the locked
blocks. `talk-storyboard` owns the feeling those blocks are supposed
to create, then says whether the outline is the one.

**Information without emotion is useless.** His test: how should
people feel after they hear this? A talk that only states the
takeaway trains assistants on a claim. A talk that makes the room
feel it is the version that gets quoted. `talk-storyboard` maps that
journey before you keep the outline.

**Don't write a script.** He never brings a verbatim manuscript on
stage. `talk-outline` refuses a word-for-word speech. The
file is an outline, not a teleprompter.

**Name who it is for, and who it is not.** His CFP advice is a
different game from giving the talk, but the same box shows up in
the outline: one person, their situation, what they already believe,
who should skip it. A talk for everyone is a talk for no one.

**Pair the words with a look.** Talks are audio-visual. A slide that
stays up for two minutes trains the room to read. `talk-slides`
cuts the storyboard into a markdown plan you walk one look at a
time (the slide before, this one, the one coming up), then a
PowerPoint: a cue, not the spoken line, about 15-30 seconds a
look, a visual note for a later pass, notes that point back at
the beat and the outline block. The click count from length and
pace is a guide. If reading the deck aloud is giving the talk,
the speech is on the slides.

### The rest of his list

These still belong to the job. This repo does not have skills for
them yet:

1. **Befriend organizers** — they book the next room, not other
   speakers.
2. **CFPs are their own game** — a provocative title, a pithy
   opener, the problem, the payoff, who should skip it. Not a
   summary of the talk.
3. **Rehearse transitions, if nothing else** — the click to the next
   slide is where people lose the thread.
4. **Remember, none of this matters** — you will be bad at first.
   Have fun anyway.
